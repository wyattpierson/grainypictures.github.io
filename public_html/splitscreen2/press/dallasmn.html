Infotainment for the indie film set

By Beth Pinsker
Staff Critic of The Dallas Morning News

When some of the network morning news shows tour the country looking for stories about "real" people, they get in huge bus convoys and attract lots of attention. The Independent Film Channel's series Split Screen has a used RV.

Host John Pierson and his crew are planning to pull up to the Dallas Theater Center on Saturday to visit the Dallas Video Festival, spread a little low-budget cheer and perhaps get a story or two for the show, which is like Entertainment Tonight for the independent film crowd.

Then they head out for a tour of Texas and Oklahoma, ending up at the South by Southwest Film Festival in Austin.

"We're looking for people who are doing things a little differently and more originally," says Mr. Pierson, who will be presenting clips from Split Screen and leading a discussion as well as hosting a workshop on Sunday on the common mistakes of filmmakers.

"The show is meant to be informative, but I also hope it's a little entertaining," he says. "The basic idea is that a lot of material about this world is actually more accessible to a wider audience than the films themselves. I don't mean in terms of availability, I just mean the way the stories are told."

Sample segments from Split Screen include an interview with Brooklyn neighbors Steve Buscemi and John Turturro; a filmmaker's search for a woman in a red sweater who attended one of his screenings at Sundance; and the happenings at a Canadian art-house theater that attracts hip Americans to cross the border.

The most controversial item is about a group of filmmaking students who go off into the woods to explore old witchcraft myths and never return. Their film stock is later found and developed by a lab. Is it real or all a hoax?

"People see that and are desperate to talk about it. I'm curious to see how that goes when we show it at the video festival," he says.

While Mr. Pierson hosts Split Screen and puts his particular spin on it, he says the vision of the 2-year-old show is to step back and give the air time over to filmmakers. That kind of facilitation is actually what Mr. Pierson does best as a long-standing indie film guru. He's the kind of guy who makes things happen.

He'll produce, distribute, promote, pontificate, reflect and, now, host. And while he may not exactly be a household name, even among those who would rather watch the IFC's Independent Spirit Awards than the Oscars, he's right up there with Miramax's Harvey Weinstein as a cult figure and schmoozing target Ñ and he doesn't even have much money to hand out. All he has to give is opportunity.

"We're really just looking for stories," he says. "And they cleared us to park in the lot at the Theater Center, so all you have to do is knock on the door. I think the refrigerator is going to work by then, so we can keep the drinks cold."
