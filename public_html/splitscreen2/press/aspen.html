Film Lovers can speak with the filmmakers
The Aspen Times

By Stewart Oksenhorn
Aspen Times Staff Writer

Since Laura Thielen took over as executive director of Aspen Filmfest some two years ago, a primary focus has been to heighten the festival experience by creating opportunities for interaction between filmmakers and filmgoers.

This year's Shortsfest features the presence of some 42 filmmakers and critics, who will engage in Q - and- A sessions after each film program, as well as two programs - Lunch With the Filmmakers and a panel discussion, The Power of the Moving Image: Vision, Tools and Technique - designed to increase audience-artist interaction.

Lunch With the Filmmakers is set for today, at noon at Jimmy's, An American Restaurant & Bar. The panel discussion is scheduled for Saturday, at 3:30pm, at Ute City Bar and Grill.

One topic likely to be explored at the panel discussion, moderated by filmmaker and Sundance Film Festival co-founder Lory Smith, is the state of independent filmmaking.

Likely to be an articulate voice on independent filmmaking is John Pierson. Pierson, who has had a hand in the financing and distribution of such independent success stories as Spike Lee's "She's Gotta Have It" and Kevin Smith's "Clerks," is also the author of the 1996 book "Spike, Mike Slacker's & Dykes." Pierson is the creator of "Split Screen," a television program on the Independent Film Channel that enters its second season next week.

"It's the best of times, and the worst of times" for independent filmmaking, said Pierson, who went to New York University's film school in the 1970's. "There are more made than ever because people have learned how to do it at an affordable price. They've learned from Linklater, kevin Smith and Ed ["The Brother's McMullen"] Burns. And every success story exponentially increases the number of people who think filmmaking is cool.

The increasing volume means that more good independent films are made and seen. It also means more lousy films are made.

"If you like the theory of diversity and democracy, that's great," said Pierson. "But if you look at a lot of those films, liek I do, you can get depressed. People are still making films about all the same things."

Another negative aspect to the expansion of the American independent film business, said Pierson, is that foreign films, long a respected and prolific alternative to Hollywood fare, are being shoved aside in favor of American independents.
