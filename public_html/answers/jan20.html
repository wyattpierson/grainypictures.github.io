Whither grainy pictures...
Posted by Devin Clancy - January 15th, 1998

I emphasize the small "g" in grainy. I don't mean where has your company gone or anything, but where have all the grainy film gone? It's my impression over the last couple of years that the quintessential grainy film, shot on a shoestring, probably in black & white, definitly on 16, is gone. You just don't see those raw, unbridled, balls to the wall film anymore in release. Where are they?

Now, to be fair, I'm on a limited budget, I haven't seen every film out there, and I've certainly not been to a festival recently, so I'm coming from a limited standpoint, but it seems like the films that get made or released are so much more polished than Clerks, She's Gotta Have it and other pillars of indie film history. Is this an eroneous impression? Am I just suffering from the premature deaths of Washington, D.C.'s only art house theaters? Or perhaps they are there, but not being picked up by distributors.

Now, if it is true that the grainy, self-financed, ugly but great films are extinct, is this maybe a good sign? Does it mean talented first-time directors are finally getting the attention they've deserved all along from potential sources of financing? Or does it mean that a select few with access to better resources are occupying the entire spectrum of independant film? I mean, it is in one way or another a finite market. There are only so many venues for a film to get exposure. I know film production is bigger than ever, but why is it I only seem to see low-budget films instead of no-budget films these days?

- Devin Clancy, stuck in the vacuum left by the Biograph and Key

p.s. loved the book, but I don't get IFC so I don't know about the show.

John Replies:

In the month or so that our website has been open to the public, Janet has very ably taken the lead in responding to all postings. A few questions and comments have partly engaged my interest, but yours is the first to draw me out through its simple but crucial insight (that and the use of the word "whither").

As "Spike, Mike...." explains, we chose the name Grainy Pictures as a badge of honor. When NY Times lead film critic Janet Maslin first read the book and found herself credited as the inspiration for our moniker as a result of her (over) use of that adjective to suggest the characteristics you list in your third sentence, she thought I was making fun of her. And if she'd used "grainy" while dismissing "Clerks" or "Go Fish", I might have. But she really grasped the spirit of those films and others like them, often offered her support, and only resorted to "grainy" shorthand as a sort of consumer warning. In our private dictionary, we share your definition of grainy - balls to the wall and, most of all, original. For us "Clerks" is grainy, "Brothers McMullen" is not - even though there's a lot of grain (not to mention barley and malt) on the screen.

When people want to send us their work and start off by talking about its production value (e.g. Rob Weiss and the "Big, Big, Bigger than Big Look"), I tune out. But at the same time when they talk about raw, self-financed, 16mm, cheap, B&W, one location, "just like 'Clerks' without the jokes", I grimace. If there are 900 features being produced independently in America this year, more than 500 fit your check list (maybe minus B&W). The majority, unfortunately, appear to be clones of one another and/or the inspirational sources that preceded them.

So a more intriguing question becomes, would there be a place in the theatrical world, or even at Sundance, for "She's Gotta Have It", "Slacker", "El Mariachi", "Clerks", "Brothers McMullen" now?! Every time an actor decides to direct, or an alumnus director returns, or a previously successful producer makes more features, or Parker Posey plays a lead, slots fill up. Vincent Gallo doesn't make grainy pictures, Kyra Sedgewick doesn't make grainy pictures, Larry Estes doesn't make grainy pictures....hell Christine Vachon doesn't make grainy pictures anymore. Independent film has obviously become an institution; the IFP should be renamed the IIFP when Spirit Award nominations get coverage in USA Today.

Your town, Washington DC, is a market in transition as a result of lost venues. If you lived in NYC, you would have had mid, low and no budget films (@ 100) of every size, shape, and color opening throughout 1997 - sometimes 3 or 4 at a time. And if you blinked, they were gone - not just Joe Brewster's "The Keeper" but Parker Posey in "House of Yes", not just the lesbian "Late Bloomers", but Tom DiCillo's "Box of Moonlight", not just Salvatore Stabile's idiotic "Gravesend", but Sundance faves "Myth of Fingerprints", and "Going All The Way." They were all going, going, gone with the wind - only it was more like a tiny breeze.

Before you enter the canyon of despair, I give you exhibit #1A. In the tradition that you yourself describe from "Stranger Than Paradise" to "She's Gotta Have It" to "Roger & Me" to (skipping ahead) "Welcome To The Dollhouse", last year brought us the brilliantly grainy "In The Company of Men". Do you agree? (It's in color, but it feels B&W.) Ironically I think "In The Company of Men" was the fourth best performing American Indie of the year - after "Eve's Bayou", "Chasing Amy", and "Ulee's Gold". A cause for hope, or merely the exception that proves Devin's rule?

John Pierson

P.S. There's a very wide range of visual artfulness within graininess. "She's Gotta Have It" is a beautiful film.
